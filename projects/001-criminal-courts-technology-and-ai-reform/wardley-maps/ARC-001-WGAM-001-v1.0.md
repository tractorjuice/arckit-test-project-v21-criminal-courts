## Executive Summary

The Criminal Courts Technology & AI Reform programme operates in a landscape dominated by genesis and custom-built components (16 of 30 mapped), with a doctrine maturity of only 2.1/5.0. The programme is entering the "War" phase of the Peace/War/Wonder cycle as LLM AI services industrialise, creating an urgent window to build criminal justice-specific capabilities on top of rapidly commoditising AI infrastructure. The strategic posture should be **constructive-offensive** — using Lawful Good and Neutral plays to build an open ecosystem, educate stakeholders, manage legacy inertia, and establish the UK as the global standard-setter for criminal justice AI governance.

Seven plays are recommended for immediate or near-term execution: **Open Approaches** and **Co-creation** to build the cross-agency ecosystem; **Education** and **Industrial Policy** to enable adoption; **Directed Investment** and **Experimentation** to capture genesis-stage opportunities; and **Managing Inertia** to address the programme's most critical execution risk. All recommended plays are LG or N alignment — appropriate for a UK Government programme operating under public accountability.

## Situational Assessment

### Position Analysis

- **Anchor (User Need)**: "Justice system participants receive fair, timely, and transparent criminal case outcomes" — this is a Custom-stage need (0.28 evolution) because no criminal justice system globally has achieved technology-enabled transformation at this scale.
- **Differentiating Components**: 4 Genesis components (AI Governance Framework 0.20, Defence Practitioner Portal 0.22, Bias Testing Framework 0.25, Defence Equality of Arms 0.18) and 12 Custom components. The programme's strategic value lies overwhelmingly in genesis/custom territory — these are components with no market equivalent.
- **Commodity Dependencies**: 8 Commodity components (Cloud Hosting 0.95, Managed Databases 0.90, GOV.UK Notify 0.92, GOV.UK Design System 0.78, Container Orchestration 0.82, Monitoring 0.80). These should be consumed as utility — zero competitive advantage in custom-building any of them.
- **Evolution Pressure**: LLM AI Services (0.68) are moving rapidly toward commodity (predicted 0.82 in 12 months). This compresses the window for building CJS-specific AI capabilities on top of the commoditising layer.

**Dominant Position Type**: **Genesis leader / Custom-Built strength** — the programme holds strategic advantage through novel CJS-specific components (AI governance, defence equality, bias testing) that no competitor or vendor can supply.

### Capability Assessment

- **Core Strengths**: Leveson Review mandate (unique political authority); multi-agency convening power (MoJ can convene all 5 CJS agencies); deep domain expertise in criminal justice; existing Common Platform infrastructure to build on.
- **Critical Weaknesses**: Doctrine maturity 2.1/5.0 (Phase I gaps in common language, user research); fragmented decision authority across 5 agencies; no agile delivery culture; skills gaps in AI/ML and cloud-native development.
- **Unique Assets**: Criminal justice case data (billions of records); 77,000+ case backlog creating urgency; Leveson Review recommendations providing political mandate; judicial engagement through Lead Judge for AI.
- **Constraints**: £281M budget envelope; 5-year timeline with 3-year backlog target; multi-agency governance creating structural slowness; DPA 2018 Part 3 criminal justice data restrictions; judiciary's constitutional independence (effective veto).

### Market Context

- **Competitor Positions**: No direct competitors — this is a government programme, not a market play. However, international CJS technology programmes (US DOJ, Australian AGD, Netherlands) are pursuing similar AI-assisted justice reforms. Vendors (Microsoft, AWS, Palantir, Thomson Reuters) position AI legal tech products that could become substitutes for custom-built components.
- **Market Signals**: LLM commoditisation accelerating (GPT models approaching utility pricing); legal tech market growing 20%+ annually; UK AI regulation (AI Safety Institute) creating global standards-setting opportunity; international interest in CJS AI governance frameworks.
- **Regulatory Environment**: UK GDPR/DPA 2018 Part 3; AI Playbook; ATRS; GDS Service Standard; TCoP; NCSC CAF. Heavy regulatory overlay creates barriers for non-compliant competitors but also slows internal delivery.
- **Partnership Opportunities**: Academic institutions for AI fairness research; GDS/CDDO for shared government services; international justice technology programmes for standards alignment; legal tech startups for defence practitioner tools.

### Peace/War/Wonder Phase

The CJS technology landscape is entering the **War** phase:
- LLM AI services are industrialising (product → commodity), triggering creative destruction in legal technology
- Incumbents (legacy systems, manual processes) face existential pressure
- New entrants (AI legal tech startups) can build on commodity AI infrastructure at a fraction of historical cost
- The programme must transform rapidly or risk building yesterday's architecture

The **Wonder** phase (new CJS-native digital services built on commodity AI + cloud) is visible on the 24-month horizon — this is where the genesis-stage components (AI Governance, Defence Portal) will create new value.

## Play Compatibility Analysis

### Reinforcing Combinations

| Primary Play | Compatible Play | Why They Reinforce |
|-------------|-----------------|---------------------|
| **Open Approaches** | **Co-creation** | Open standards attract cross-agency participation; co-creation builds legitimacy that accelerates open adoption. Ubuntu case study: open source + community co-creation = dominant cloud OS. |
| **Open Approaches** | **Education** | Education explains the value of open standards to sceptical agencies; open standards give education tangible artefacts to demonstrate. |
| **Experimentation** | **Directed Investment** | Experiments identify which genesis components warrant scaled investment. Investment resources are concentrated on proven experiments, not speculative bets. Amazon case study: experiments + directed investment = AWS. |
| **Industrial Policy** | **Managing Inertia** | Ministerial mandate provides the authority to overcome agency inertia. Without industrial policy, inertia management relies solely on persuasion; with it, mandatory adoption is available as escalation path. |
| **Education** | **Co-creation** | Educating judiciary about AI builds the trust needed for co-creation of the AI Governance Framework. Co-creation produces artefacts that become education materials for the next wave. |
| **Refactoring** | **Managing Inertia** | Refactoring Common Platform addresses the sunk cost inertia directly — transforming the toxic component into a strategic asset rather than abandoning it (which would trigger political inertia). |

### Strategic Thrust

The six reinforcing combinations form two coherent strategic thrusts:

1. **Ecosystem Building** (Open Approaches + Co-creation + Education + Industrial Policy): Build an open, co-created CJS ecosystem with government mandate as the backstop.
2. **Capability Development** (Experimentation + Directed Investment + Managing Inertia + Refactoring): Rapidly develop genesis-stage capabilities while removing legacy barriers.

### Conflicting Plays

| Play A | Play B | Conflict | Resolution |
|--------|--------|----------|------------|
| Open Approaches (publish CJS standards openly) | Industrial Policy (mandate adoption by government authority) | Tension between voluntary open adoption and mandatory government mandate. Open approaches work by attraction; industrial policy works by compulsion. | **Sequence**: Lead with Open Approaches (publish standards, invite contribution, build legitimacy). Use Industrial Policy as escalation when voluntary adoption stalls at specific agencies. The standard is open; the adoption requirement is mandatory. This is coherent — like building codes that adopt open engineering standards but mandate compliance. |

No other conflicts identified. The play portfolio is strategically coherent — all LG/N alignment, all reinforcing through the two strategic thrusts.

## Selected Plays — Detailed Execution

### Play 1: Open Approaches (LG) — Category B: Accelerators

**Description**: Publish Criminal Justice Data Standards, API specifications, and integration patterns as genuinely open artefacts to accelerate cross-agency adoption and establish the UK as the global standard-setter for CJS interoperability.

**Why it applies here**: CJS Data Standards (evolution 0.28) and Cross-Agency Integration Platform (0.35) are custom-built components that need to move toward product maturity. Open publication accelerates this by inviting contribution from all 5 agencies, reducing the perception that standards are imposed by MoJ, and creating reuse opportunities for devolved administrations and international partners. TCoP Points 3 and 4 mandate open standards.

**Prerequisites**:
- CJS Data Standards working group established with cross-agency representation
- Draft data dictionary covering the 50 most critical terms completed
- Legal confirmation that standards can be published under Open Government Licence

**Execution Steps**:
1. Publish v0.1 draft CJS Data Standards on GitHub (public repository under MoJ organisation) within 60 days
2. Invite contributions from all 5 agencies, legal tech vendors, academic institutions, and international CJS programmes
3. Conduct open consultation on API specification for first 3 integration points (police-CPS, CPS-HMCTS, HMCTS-HMPPS)
4. Publish v1.0 standard on data.gov.uk with Open Government Licence within 6 months
5. Present standard at international forum (UNODC, Five Eyes justice technology group) to establish UK leadership

**Expected Outcomes**:
- **Short-term (0-3 months)**: Draft standard published; contributions from 3+ agencies; first external pull requests
- **Long-term (6-18 months)**: Standard adopted by all 5 agencies; referenced by devolved administrations; international recognition

**Risks and Mitigations**:

| Risk | Likelihood | Mitigation |
|------|------------|------------|
| Agencies resist open publication (security concerns about CJS data structures) | M | Separate schema definition (open) from data content (protected). Security review before publication. |
| Standard becomes fragmented by competing agency proposals | M | MoJ CDIO as standard owner with clear governance. Consensus-seek first, MoJ-decides if deadlocked. |
| International programmes publish competing standards first | L | Move fast. v0.1 draft within 60 days. First-mover advantage in global CJS standards space. |

**Success Criteria**:
- [ ] CJS Data Standards v0.1 published on GitHub within 60 days
- [ ] Contributions received from 3+ agencies within 90 days
- [ ] v1.0 standard adopted by all 5 agencies within 12 months
- [ ] At least 1 international programme references the standard within 18 months

**Review Points**: 60 days (v0.1 published?), 6 months (v1.0 ready?), 12 months (adoption rate?)

### Play 2: Education (LG) — Category A: User Perception

**Description**: Systematically educate judiciary, defence practitioners, and CJS stakeholders about AI capabilities, governance safeguards, and the programme's approach to equality of arms — building the trust and understanding required for adoption.

**Why it applies here**: Doctrine assessment identifies "Know Your Users" at 2/5. Judiciary holds constitutional veto over case-affecting AI. Defence practitioners are the most sceptical and least-resourced user group. Education must precede deployment — without trust, even excellent technology will be rejected. The AI Governance Framework (genesis, 0.20) and Defence Practitioner Portal (genesis, 0.22) cannot succeed without educated, engaged users.

**Prerequisites**:
- AI disclosure review MVP operational (even in sandbox)
- AI Governance Framework draft published
- Judicial steering group established

**Execution Steps**:
1. Design targeted education programmes for 4 audiences: judiciary (constitutional safeguards focus), prosecutors (efficiency and quality focus), defence (equality of arms and access focus), victims (transparency and tracking focus)
2. Conduct judicial education sessions with Lead Judge for AI — demonstrate AI disclosure sandbox, explain governance controls, address independence concerns
3. Create defence practitioner education programme through CBA and Law Society channels — demonstrate Defence Portal prototype, explain LAA-funded access model
4. Develop plain-language materials explaining AI governance (ATRS, DPIA, human-in-the-loop) for non-technical stakeholders
5. Establish ongoing education cadence (quarterly updates) as AI capabilities evolve

**Expected Outcomes**:
- **Short-term (0-3 months)**: Judicial steering group endorses AI disclosure pilot approach; CBA/Law Society agree to promote defence practitioner engagement
- **Long-term (6-18 months)**: Judicial approval for AI disclosure in live cases; 50%+ defence practitioner awareness of portal; public confidence in AI governance

**Risks and Mitigations**:

| Risk | Likelihood | Mitigation |
|------|------------|------------|
| Judiciary rejects AI in criminal proceedings despite education | M | Focus on augmentation narrative (AI assists, human decides). Start with low-risk use cases (transcription before disclosure). |
| Defence profession disengages due to legal aid constraints | H | LAA-funded access model. CBA/Law Society partnership. Mobile-first design for sole practitioners. |

**Success Criteria**:
- [ ] Judicial steering group meets at least 3 times in first 6 months
- [ ] 50+ defence practitioners participate in portal prototype testing
- [ ] Judicial approval obtained for AI disclosure pilot at 1+ Crown Court sites

**Review Points**: 3 months (judicial engagement?), 6 months (defence participation?), 9 months (pilot approval?)

### Play 3: Experimentation (LG) — Category G: Attacking

**Description**: Launch rapid, low-cost experiments for genesis-stage components to learn from real deployment rather than further planning. Accept high failure rates. Prioritise learning velocity over production readiness.

**Why it applies here**: Doctrine maturity shows "Bias Towards Action" at 2/5 — the programme has produced 16+ governance artefacts but zero operational deployments. For genesis-stage components (AI Governance 0.20, Defence Portal 0.22, Bias Testing 0.25), the biggest risk is not failure — it is learning nothing because no experiment was run. LLM AI Services (0.68, moving to 0.82) provide commodity infrastructure for rapid prototyping.

**Prerequisites**:
- Cloud hosting provisioned (G-Cloud procurement, 2-week lead time)
- LLM AI services available (Azure OpenAI or AWS Bedrock via G-Cloud)
- At least 1 Crown Court site willing to participate in sandbox pilot

**Execution Steps**:
1. **Week 1-4**: Deploy AI disclosure sandbox at 1 Crown Court site using existing LLM services. Process anonymised historical cases. Measure accuracy, false positive rate, time savings.
2. **Week 1-4 (parallel)**: Build Defence Portal clickable prototype. Test with 10 defence practitioners recruited through CBA. Measure: can they complete 5 key tasks? What breaks?
3. **Week 4-8**: Deploy AI transcription experiment in 2 magistrates' courts (lower risk than Crown Court). Measure: accuracy vs. human transcription; cost per hearing hour; user satisfaction.
4. **Week 8-12**: Build AI Governance sandbox — register test AI tools in ATRS, run DPIA process for sandbox, test judicial approval workflow end-to-end.
5. **Month 3**: Publish lessons-learned report. Feed into full programme design.

**Expected Outcomes**:
- **Short-term (0-3 months)**: 4 experiments completed; evidence of AI efficacy for CJS; validated (or invalidated) assumptions; user feedback from real practitioners
- **Long-term (6-18 months)**: Experiments inform full-scale design; validated components move from genesis to custom-built; failed experiments save millions in avoided misallocated investment

**Risks and Mitigations**:

| Risk | Likelihood | Mitigation |
|------|------------|------------|
| Experiment reveals AI is ineffective for CJS disclosure | L | Still valuable — saves £50M+ by discovering this before full-scale build. Pivot to alternative approach. |
| Experiment generates adverse media coverage ("government tests AI on criminal cases") | M | Clear communications strategy. Frame as "safety testing before deployment". Emphasise human-in-the-loop. |
| Experiment scope creeps into production deployment | M | Strict sandbox boundaries. Named experiment owner with authority to stop. 12-week maximum per experiment. |

**Success Criteria**:
- [ ] First AI disclosure experiment operational within 30 days of cloud provisioning
- [ ] 4 experiments running within 90 days
- [ ] Lessons-learned report published at month 3
- [ ] At least 1 experiment produces evidence sufficient to inform full-scale design decision

**Review Points**: 30 days (first experiment live?), 60 days (all experiments running?), 90 days (lessons learned?)

### Play 4: Managing Inertia (N) — Category F: Defensive

**Description**: Actively identify, name, and manage the organisational resistance that will prevent the programme from executing its strategy. Focus on the three most dangerous inertia sources: Common Platform sunk cost bias, legacy system entrenchment, and multi-agency governance friction.

**Why it applies here**: Doctrine assessment scores Manage Inertia at 2/5 — the programme recognises inertia but does not manage it. The Wardley Map identifies 6 inertia factors rated Medium to Critical. Inertia is the single most common reason strategically correct plays fail in execution. Without this play, Open Approaches, Experimentation, and Directed Investment will all stall against organisational resistance.

**Prerequisites**:
- Programme board acknowledges inertia as a first-order delivery risk (not just a line in the risk register)
- MoJ Permanent Secretary (SRO) willing to escalate to ministerial level when agencies resist

**Execution Steps**:
1. **Name the inertia explicitly**: Present inertia analysis to programme board using Wardley inertia types (success, capital, financial, political, skills, supplier, consumer). Quantify cost of inaction for each.
2. **Appoint cross-agency change champions**: One senior person per agency (HMCTS, CPS, HMPPS, LAA, police) with authority to commit their agency to change and escalation path to their CEO/DG.
3. **Address Common Platform sunk cost**: Commission independent assessment of Common Platform's strategic fitness. Publish findings honestly. Decide: stabilise-and-extend OR plan replacement. Either decision is better than ambiguity.
4. **Create legacy migration incentive**: Offer agencies that migrate early a share of the saved maintenance budget for investment in new capabilities. Make migration rewarding, not just mandatory.
5. **Establish ministerial escalation path**: Define circumstances under which the programme can escalate agency non-cooperation to the Lord Chancellor's office. Use rarely, but its existence changes behaviour.

**Expected Outcomes**:
- **Short-term (0-3 months)**: Inertia named and acknowledged at programme board; change champions appointed; Common Platform assessment commissioned
- **Long-term (6-18 months)**: First 5 legacy applications migrated; Common Platform evolution strategy agreed; cross-agency integration progressing without governance deadlock

**Risks and Mitigations**:

| Risk | Likelihood | Mitigation |
|------|------------|------------|
| Agencies perceive inertia management as MoJ overreach | H | Frame as shared problem, not MoJ directive. Change champions are agency-nominated, not MoJ-appointed. |
| Common Platform assessment reveals fundamental unfitness | M | Better to know now than discover during integration. Have contingency plan ready. |
| Ministerial escalation damages cross-agency relationships | L | Use only as last resort. Document escalation criteria in advance. Ensure agencies know the path exists before it is used. |

**Success Criteria**:
- [ ] Inertia analysis presented to programme board within 30 days
- [ ] Change champions appointed (1 per agency) within 60 days
- [ ] Common Platform assessment complete within 90 days
- [ ] First agency completes a legacy application migration within 12 months

**Review Points**: 30 days (board presentation?), 60 days (champions appointed?), 6 months (migration progress?)

### Play 5: Directed Investment (LG) — Category G: Attacking

**Description**: Concentrate investment resources on the two highest-leverage genesis/custom components that define the programme's strategic value: AI Disclosure Review and Cross-Agency Integration Platform. Resist the temptation to spread investment across all 30 components simultaneously.

**Why it applies here**: The programme scope includes AI disclosure, defence portal, legacy migration, victim tracking, cross-agency integration, and AI governance — all simultaneously. Doctrine assessment identifies "Be Pragmatic" at 3/5 but programme scope is the opposite of pragmatic. Directed investment means choosing where to win first. AI Disclosure Review (evolution 0.25) is the core differentiator tied to BR-001 (backlog reduction). Cross-Agency Integration Platform (0.35) is the keystone that all three value chains depend on.

**Prerequisites**:
- Experimentation play produces evidence on AI disclosure efficacy (Play 3)
- Programme board agrees to prioritise 2 components over simultaneous delivery of all

**Execution Steps**:
1. Allocate 60% of Year 1 delivery budget to AI Disclosure Review and Cross-Agency Integration Platform
2. Procure DOS Outcomes for AI Disclosure (£5-8M) and Integration Platform (£3-5M) as the first two delivery lots
3. Defer Defence Practitioner Portal to Year 2 alpha (user research in Year 1 only)
4. Defer legacy migration beyond assessment phase to Year 2 (migrate the highest-risk 5 applications in Year 1 only)
5. Track investment concentration: report to programme board the % of spend allocated to top-2 vs. other components
